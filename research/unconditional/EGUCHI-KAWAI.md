# Eguchi–Kawai large-N volume independence — assessment

> 2026-11-23 · Fresh-framework probe. **Verdict: a genuinely different limiting procedure (N→∞ at fixed volume → one site) that could in principle sit at physical 't Hooft coupling — but its load-bearing claim (volume independence) is an unproven large-N statement, its one wall is the same center-symmetry breaking as small-S¹, and a one-site matrix integral carries no Euclidean time, hence no mass gap as a spectral object. Relocates the wall; moves no distance.**

**(a) Precise statement; what is proven.** Eguchi–Kawai (PRL 48 (1982) 1063): for SU(N) lattice YM on T⁴ at fixed 't Hooft coupling λ=g²N, the large-N loop (Schwinger–Dyson) equations coincide with those of the *one-site / one-plaquette* matrix model, so all ⟨W(C)⟩ and the free energy match — **iff the center Z_N⁴ is unbroken**, i.e. the d momentum link matrices have uniform eigenvalue density. None of this is a theorem; it is 't Hooft's planar reduction argument. It holds order-by-order in perturbation theory and is confirmed numerically in center-symmetric phases (twisted/deformed EK); it **fails** when center symmetry breaks — Bhanot–Heller–Neuberger (PLB 113 (1982) 47) found the weak-coupling one-site model has a Z_N⁴-breaking transition. Modern framing: Kovtun–Ünsal–Yaffe (JHEP 0706:019 (2007)) "large-N volume independence" — still a physics statement, not a proof. The obstacle is **exactly** the small-S¹ obstacle (GPY center breaking), stated in the volume instead of the circle.

**(b) Can center symmetry be kept unbroken rigorously?** No proof exists in any scheme.
- *Twist* (Gonzalez-Arroyo–Okawa, PRD 27 (1983) 2397; 't Hooft NPB 153 (1979) 141): 't Hooft flux makes the eigenvalue density uniform via a flat connection; expected to preserve Z_N⁴ to all orders and the cleanest construction — but the all-orders symmetry is assumed, not proven.
- *Quench* (BHN): quenches the eigenvalues — nonlocal, non-unitary, no reflection positivity; an ad hoc device, not a theorem.
- *Double-trace deformation* (Shifman–Ünsal 0802.1232; Ünsal–Yaffe 0803.0344): center-symmetric by construction, but that it dynamically fixes the vacuum and is "adiabatic" is conjectural — and this is *literally the small-S¹ bion technology*.

So (b) merges into (R1)'s open step: no rigorous center stabilization at strong/continuum coupling.

**(c) Physical coupling?** EK is formulated at fixed λ with no weak-coupling expansion, so *in principle* it targets the 't Hooft regime directly — its one real conceptual advantage over bions (no NΛL≪1, no marginal bion). But the equivalence *is* the conjecture, and the regime that matters — large β, continuum — is precisely where center symmetry breaks (the EK transition). TEK/deformations postpone it; none proves survival into the continuum. So it does **not** provably reach physical coupling.

**(d) Finite-matrix gap provable; N=3 transfer?** The one-site EK model is a **0D matrix integral**: no transfer matrix, no Euclidean time, no Källén–Lehmann measure — so "the gap of the finite matrix model" is not a spectral quantity at all. At best one reads a gap off the decay of the equivalent 4D loop correlators, but proving that decay (area law / exponential clustering) inside the matrix integral is an unsolved large-N integral problem, and no area-law theorem exists. Even granting an N=∞ gap, transfer to N=3 has no theorem: volume independence is strictly an N=∞ statement and is known to fail at finite N; 1/N corrections are uncontrolled (cf. H1-T8). So no.

**Genuinely new vs R1/R2?** The limiting procedure differs (N→∞ at fixed volume vs small-L semiclassics) and the dilute-gas / marginal-bion obstruction is absent — a real structural difference. But the load-bearing wall is the *same* center-symmetry breaking, fixed by the *same* double-trace/twist tools, and EK adds two fresh, equally hard obligations (strong-coupling volume independence; N=∞→3 transfer) plus the loss of a time direction to host the gap. Net: it relocates center symmetry and adds the N=3 transfer wall; it is not a new route to the conditional-theorem gap.
